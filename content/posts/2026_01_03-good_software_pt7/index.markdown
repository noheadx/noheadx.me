Introduction



This series began with a simple point: what makes good software ‘good’ now means different things to different people.



Some software is built to last. Some is built to solve a problem quickly. Both are valid. Confusing them is costly.



This final article synthesizes the series and clarifies the leadership implications.



⸻



Two Definitions, One Decision



Across the series, two categories have emerged consistently:



Durable software

    •   long-lived

    •   multi-team

    •   structurally constrained

    •   intentionally governed



Disposable software

    •   short-lived

    •   locally owned

    •   loosely constrained

    •   intentionally temporary



The failure mode is not choosing a category - it is not choosing at all.



⸻



Leadership Is About Intent



Good software does not emerge from frameworks, tooling, or velocity.



It emerges from intent.



Leadership must provide:

    •   clarity of purpose

    •   boundaries of responsibility

    •   permission to stop

    •   discipline to remove



Management executes within these constraints. Engineering builds within them. AI accelerates the teams within the constraints.



Without intent, acceleration only magnifies chaos.



⸻



The Central Pattern



Across all articles, one pattern repeats:

    •   unclear leadership intent

    •   local optimization

    •   hidden debt

    •   delayed recognition

    •   expensive correction



This pattern is not new. AI only makes it faster.



⸻



The Questions Leaders Must Ask



Before approving any software initiative, leaders should be able to answer:

    1.  What kind of software is this?

    2.  How long should it live?

    3.  Who owns it over time?

    4.  What happens when it fails?

    5.  How does it end?



If these questions feel uncomfortable, that discomfort is a signal.



⸻



Closing



Good software in 2025 is not about elegance or cleverness; it's about functionality and responsibility.



Leadership is the act of consciously choosing consequences.

Management is the act of executing those choices.



AI does not change this distinction.

It only removes the illusion that we can avoid it.
