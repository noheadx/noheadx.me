## Introduction

Many organizations claim to have a software strategy, but few actually have a plan for the full lifecycle of their systems.

Most systems are built without a clear answer to three fundamental questions:
 * Should we build this ourselves?
 * Should we buy this capability?
 * Should we treat this as disposable?

It might seem flexible to avoid these decisions at first, but over time, this leads to confusion in your architecture and extra work for your teams.

This article focuses on leadership practices that make lifecycle decisions explicit and enforceable.

⸻

## Building is a Long-Term Commitment

Building software isn’t just about creating something new. It’s about taking on long-term responsibility for what you build.

When leaders decide to build software in-house, they also agree to:
 * long-term ownership
 * maintenance cost
 * skill retention
 * security responsibility
 * integration responsibility

These commitments outlive roadmaps and reorganizations.

Leadership must therefore reserve “build” decisions for systems that:
 * differentiate the organization
 * require deep domain integration
 * cannot be sourced reliably
 * justify long-term investment

Any other situation should be carefully reviewed.

⸻

## Buy Is Not Abdication

People often think buying software means handing off responsibility, but really, it just changes what you’re responsible for.

Buying requires leadership clarity around:
 * vendor risk
 * integration boundaries
 * exit strategies
 * data ownership
 * operational dependencies

Buying poorly is as dangerous as building poorly.

Leadership must treat buy decisions as architectural decisions, not procurement exercises.

⸻

## Disposal Is a Valid Option

Most organizations don’t see disposal as a real option. Old systems stick around because removing them can feel like admitting defeat.

But in fact, choosing to dispose of a system shows maturity.

Leadership must normalize:
 * sunsetting systems
 * decommissioning tools
 * deleting code
 * removing platforms that no longer serve their purpose

If you never get rid of old systems, your architecture gets bigger, not better.

⸻

## The Cost of Deferred Decisions

When leadership avoids making lifecycle decisions, teams try to make up for it by making small, local fixes:
 * patching instead of redesigning
 * extending instead of replacing
 * maintaining systems that should not exist

Over time, this makes it harder for the organization to make clear decisions.

## Summary

A good software strategy is not about choosing technology, but about selecting lifecycles.

Leadership must:
 * decide explicitly
 * communicate clearly
 * revisit decisions regularly
 * remove systems without nostalgia

Clarity beats flexibility, always.
